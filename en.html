Guide 1 · Events

How to invite influencers to an event properly

An event invitation is the first piece of content your event produces. Creators decide whether your launch is worth their evening — and their audience's attention — from the invitation itself. Here is how invitations that get accepted are built:

Start with the right list, not the biggest one

  • Fit before followers. Category, city and audience come first; reach is the last filter, not the first. A beauty creator at a fintech launch helps no one.
  • Check what they post at events. Some creators are brilliant at events — stories, live moments, next-day posts. Others attend and never post. Their past event coverage tells you which is which.
  • Invite with a reason. "We follow your café reviews and this opening is in your neighbourhood" gets a yes. A mass-forwarded invitation gets ignored.

Send it early, and make it complete

One message containing what, where and when — plus what is in it for the guest: a first look, an exclusive interview, the product before its release. Then the practicalities: parking or valet, dress code, plus-one policy, and a named contact for the day. Creators plan content around their week; a vague "save the date" with details "coming soon" usually loses to a complete invitation.

Run the day for coverage

  • A moment worth filming. One designed visual moment — a reveal, a hands-on demo, a tasting — gives every guest the same strong shot instead of a logo wall.
  • The product in their hands. Guests should leave having tried the thing, not just seen it. Trial is what turns attendance into content.
  • No posting demands at the door. PR invitations do not come with deliverable ultimatums; any formal coverage is agreed separately and in advance. What you can do is make posting easy — good light, the product ready, the brand's handles written correctly so tagging takes seconds.

Follow up like a person

A thank-you message the next day, the official photos and video shared with them for their own edits, and an honest note of who posted what. The relationship you build at this event is the guest list of the next one.

Guide 2 · Seeding

Product seeding that actually gets posted

Seeding fails when it is treated as shipping. It works when it is treated as a first experience of the brand. Nobody owes you a post for a parcel — creators post what genuinely fits their content and their audience. Here is how to earn the post:

Pick fewer, better-matched creators

  • Category fit is everything. A skincare creator for a skincare product sounds obvious, yet most seeding waste is products sprayed at generic "lifestyle" lists.
  • Look for organic product content. Creators who already review, unbox or recommend things in your category post gifted products naturally; creators who never do will not start with yours.
  • Mix sizes deliberately. A few larger names for reach, more mid-size and micro creators for a volume of honest posts — mid-size creators often post gifted products more reliably because their audience relationship is closer.

Design the unboxing

The parcel is opened on camera or not at all. Packaging that opens well, the product visible immediately, one short personal note — handwritten-style, naming the creator and one specific thing about their content — and a single clear card: what the product is, the one thing to try, and where to tag. Long press kits get skimmed; a considered box gets filmed.

Time it to a moment

Seed against a launch, a season, a restock — something the creator can hook a post to. A product that arrives with a reason to talk about it now outperforms one that arrives "whenever". And ship early enough that the trial happens before the moment, not after it.

Never attach strings — and always follow up

No "post by Friday or send it back" messages; that is an ad buy pretending to be a gift, and creators can tell instantly. Do follow up once, warmly, after delivery: confirm it arrived, offer help or a shade or size swap, and stay available. Track who posted — and thank them publicly when they do.

Guide 3 · Briefing

How to brief an influencer for a launch

A launch brief has one job: make every creator's post different in voice and identical in facts. Too loose and the launch story fragments; too tight and you get five copies of the same ad. The balance is a short written brief with these parts:

The facts that must land

  • What is launching, and when. The product name spelled exactly, the launch date, and where it will be sold.
  • The one-line story. Why this product exists, in one sentence a creator can say in their own words.
  • Two or three proof points. The ingredient, the feature, the result — claims you can back up, phrased the way they may safely be said.

What to leave open

The hook, the format, the setting, the order of the story. A creator knows whether their audience responds to a morning routine, a first-impression test or an honest comparison — let them choose the vehicle; you supply the cargo.

The practical page

  • Timing. An embargo or go-live window, and whether posts are scheduled or "post when ready" inside a range.
  • Deliverables and usage. How many posts, which formats — and if you plan to reuse the content in ads, the usage scope agreed in writing now, not after the post performs.
  • Must-avoids. Competitor names, restricted claims for your category, anything legal has flagged.
  • One contact. A single named person for questions, with a response time creators can rely on during launch week.

The mistake to avoid

Sending the brief the night before launch day. Creators build content around their own calendars; a brief that arrives with production time gets produced properly. The same brief at the last minute gets a rushed post — or none at all.

05 — FAQ

What brands ask before their first PR campaign

What is influencer PR?

It builds a brand's presence inside creators' content in a natural, credible way — coverage, product experiences, event attendance and organic mentions — instead of direct paid advertising. Real people talk about the brand to an audience that trusts them.

How is influencer PR different from paid influencer campaigns?

A paid campaign buys specific posts; PR builds a relationship and an experience. The creator tries the product, attends the launch or travels with the brand, and the content comes out in their own voice. PR is stronger for credibility and reputation; paid is stronger for direct messages and quick sales — many brands need both.

What is product seeding?

It is sending the product to carefully selected creators as a complete experience — packaging, a personal note, the right timing — so the creator shares their experience if they genuinely like it. The most credible form of coverage, because it comes from a real trial, not an ad.
